# Website feedback: two buyer perspectives

Synthesized from two persona reads:
- **A** = SME business leader / CFO: budget authority, risk-conscious, ROI-oriented
- **B** = Women founder seeking a CTO: non-technical or semi-technical, trust-seeking, has been burned before

Both personas read the full site independently. This document maps what each found, where they overlap, and what to actually do about it.

---

## What's working (don't break it)

- **Tagline: "we hold the complexity, so you can lead"** — lands for both personas. Keep it exactly.
- **No lock-in, no black boxes** — A trusts it immediately. B has been burned by the opposite. Name it once prominently and don't walk it back.
- **Product ladder (sparring → deep dive → dev cycle)** — both personas valued the low-stakes on-ramp. A sees a clear process. B sees permission to not know yet.
- **Range section depth** — the breadth of domains and the featured tags (Hobbit, election forecasting, drone algorithm) create genuine credibility. This is underleveraged, not over-stated.
- **The name** — B noticed it immediately and it did emotional work before she'd read anything. A is neutral-to-curious. Keep it.

---

## Problem 1: The case studies tell one client's story three times

**What both personas see:**
Skyseed appears in 3 of 4 project blocks. A reads this as concentrated risk — one anchor client, limited business development track record. B reads it as: *have they worked with someone like me?*

**The actual problem:**
The projects section isn't demonstrating breadth of client relationships — it's demonstrating depth with one relationship. That's a different (and weaker) signal for a new buyer.

**What to do:**
- Cap Skyseed at one case study. Make it the strongest one (the ERP / process digitization story — it has the clearest transformation arc).
- Replace the other two Skyseed slots with different clients or a composite anonymized case. Even "a Berlin-based startup in health tech" is better than a third Skyseed entry.
- Prioritize: one early-stage founder story, one SME/established org story. That covers both buyer personas directly.

---

## Problem 2: Case studies have no numbers

**What A sees:**
"What used to require human effort now just runs" is narrative. A needs a number to carry into a decision. Something replaced, saved, automated — give it a unit.

**What B sees:**
B is less ROI-focused but numbers would still help her calibrate: *is this the scale of project I could afford? Is this the kind of outcome I could expect?*

**What to do:**
For each case study, add one concrete outcome. It doesn't need to be financial. Examples:
- "replaced 4 separate tools with one system"
- "what took 3 days of manual work now runs overnight"
- "handles the full operational workflow for a team of 25"
- "leads now arrive pre-qualified — sales cycle cut in half"

One sentence per project. Specificity is credibility.

---

## Problem 3: The two-person capacity question is unanswered

**What A sees:**
Before signing a maintainer contract, A is asking: what's my risk if one of you is unavailable? You address the upside of being two people but not the bus factor.

**What B sees:**
B is less worried about this explicitly, but the tandem framing makes her wonder about her place in the relationship dynamic — will she always get both of you? Either of you?

**What to do:**
Add one sentence in the about section or services section that directly names this and answers it. Something like: *working as a tandem means you always have continuity — if one of us is unavailable, the other knows your system and can keep things moving.* Don't make it a long paragraph. Just close the loop.

---

## Problem 4: "find out if we vibe" undersells the sparring session

**What A sees:**
A has fought for budget authority and organizational credibility. "Vibe" reads as unprofessional and makes A wonder whether the working relationship will have enough structure.

**What B sees:**
B actually doesn't mind the word, but she notices the sparring description focuses on *you* finding out if you like each other — when what she wants to know is: *will you take my situation seriously and give me something useful?*

**What to do:**
Rewrite the sparring description to lead with what the client gets out of it, not the relationship-testing framing. Keep it light in tone — just shift the subject from "we" to "you":

> *bring your situation, your stuck point, your half-formed idea. you leave with clear thinking and concrete next steps — and a real sense of whether we're the right fit.*

---

## Problem 5: "nobody should feel judged for not knowing yet" appears twice

**What A sees:**
Once is reassuring. Twice makes A feel like the intended audience is more naive than A considers themselves to be. Second occurrence slightly undermines credibility.

**What B sees:**
B finds it genuinely relieving both times — but even B notices the repetition and wonders if it's a verbal tic rather than a considered value.

**What to do:**
Keep it once. The contact section is the better placement — it's where the ask lives, so the reassurance is most useful there. In the services section, replace the second instance with something that expresses the same value differently — perhaps frame it around *your* approach to the work rather than the client's state of knowledge.

---

## Problem 6: The bios read like LinkedIn summaries

**What A sees:**
A wants to know: are these people reliable? Do they communicate clearly? Have they handled situations like mine before? The bios answer none of this.

**What B sees:**
B wants to feel the people behind the company. She's choosing a working relationship, not a vendor. "Warmth and can-do attitude" and "calm structure and crystal-clear thinking" are adjectives anyone could put on a website. She wants a flash of something real — a sentence that couldn't have been written by anyone else.

**What to do:**
Each bio needs one sentence that is specific and only true of that person. Not a skill list. Not personality adjectives. Something that shows a point of view or a way of being in the work. The current bios can stay as a base — just add one grounding, specific sentence per person that couldn't be copy-pasted onto anyone else's website.

---

## Problem 7: Founders are listed but not spoken to

**What A sees:**
No issue — A is the SME owner, not the founder. This gap doesn't affect A.

**What B sees:**
B is in the audience list ("SMEs, technical teams, and founders") but the rest of the site doesn't speak to the specific experience of being a founder. Founders aren't just small SMEs — they're building something from nothing, navigating uncertainty, and looking for a technical partner who can hold ambiguity and strategic unknowns, not just execute specs.

**What to do:**
Add one explicit signal — somewhere in services or about — that shows you understand the founder experience as distinct. Not a long section. One sentence would do it: something about working with people who are still figuring out what they're building, not just people who have a clear spec ready.

---

## Problem 8: No pricing signal creates different anxieties for different buyers

**What A sees:**
A can't pre-qualify budget fit before booking. Some CFOs won't engage without a ballpark.

**What B sees:**
Pricing silence reads as *we work with serious clients with real budgets* — and B isn't sure she qualifies yet.

**What to do:**
You don't need a price list. You need one sentence that gives buyers permission to reach out regardless of where they are. Options:
- Name a floor: "projects start from X" (only if you want to filter)
- Name the range reality: "we work across scales — from a focused one-week sprint to a long-term partnership"
- Explicitly invite the uncertain: "not sure if your budget fits? ask anyway"

The third option is the most consistent with the rest of your tone and serves B the most directly.

---

## Problem 9: The tone has two registers that don't fully cohere

**What A sees:**
Hollywood rendering engines + witch cat video = confusing. The technical credentials suggest serious depth; the whimsy suggests the opposite. A can't quite tell which one is the real signal.

**What B sees:**
B loves the name and finds the witch cat charming. But she also worries: *are they too cool for my unglamorous B2B problem?* The Range section's featured projects (The Hobbit, election forecasting) are so impressive they accidentally raise the bar on what B thinks you want to work on.

**What to do:**
This is a tension to manage rather than eliminate. The whimsy is part of the brand identity — don't sand it off. But the Range section needs one ordinary project alongside the impressive ones. Something like "internal tool for a 10-person team" or "invoice automation for a small business" would signal that you're just as invested in the small, real, unglamorous problem. This costs nothing in the "impressive" department but buys a lot of accessibility.

---

## Problem 10: No social proof from clients

**What both personas see:**
There are no testimonials, no quotes, no named reactions from people who've worked with you. A wants to de-risk the decision. B wants to see herself in someone who's been here before her.

**What to do:**
One testimonial would change the feel of the entire site. Priority order for maximum impact:
1. A woman founder, early stage — serves B directly and signals values alignment
2. Skyseed (you clearly have a long relationship — there's something quotable there)
3. Any client, any sector

It doesn't need to be long. Two sentences from a real person outweigh three paragraphs of copy.
